Some background about OCR: Technologies that try to “recognize” typewritten or handwritten text characters and transform them into machine-readable text, speech, punch cards, or any other format we need have been around since the early 20th century. The goals have usually been to make interpretation of text more efficient, for instance automating data entry, or for accessibility, often to make text accessible to people who can’t see it. If you have a machine that can recognize individual text characters or whole words from a page, then that information can be used for a lot of different applications. Many of the earliest OCR-type technologies were intended to make written materials accessible to people with visual impairments, recognizing text and transforming it into audio tones, speech, or something tactile. But businesses and government agencies were also early adopters of OCR machines that helped to interpret, record, and tally large amounts of information. The United States Postal Service started using OCR around the 1960s to help sort mail automatically, for instance, and retail businesses started using it to scan price tags.

Archivists have been using OCR technology since at least the 1980s. Earlier OCR technologies were best used for typewritten text, preferably regularly spaced and with high contrast between the text and the page, but a lot of archival material is handwritten, messily formatted, on deteriorating paper, and so on. OCR technology breakthroughs in the 1980s made it more interesting to archivists - it could do more with handwriting, especially cursive handwriting, and with low resolution and deteriorating documents. OCR offered the chance to make archival records both more accessible and more searchable, by transforming information on the page into digital text. OCR machines were also becoming smaller and less expensive, making them a more realistic option for archival institutions with smaller budgets.

In the mid-1980s, the National Archives and Records Administration tested an OCR machine called the “Workless Station 213-A”, manufactured by the DEST Corporation in California, which they attached to an IBM microcomputer. They used it to create machine-readable versions of printed archival finding aids, which it did successfully - less than 10% error rate. 

In the early 2000s, online OCR services started popping up, and more applications, such as Adobe Acrobat, started incorporating OCR. The accuracy, speed, ease of use, and variety of applications of OCR tools have only improved over the past couple of decades.

OCR technology has improved significantly since archivists really became interested in it in the 1980s, for digitizing printed finding aids and archival materials. Archivists still value it primarily because it can offer access to archival documents for people with visual impairments or who otherwise cannot directly access the original source, and because it makes original sources keyword searchable. But the technology has never been perfect, and as with all new and changing technologies, archivists must continually assess how OCR technologies fits with our longstanding professional values and code of ethics. The archival profession has existed since at least the early 19th century - archival practices and values have changed quite a lot over the past 200 years, but the technologies available to us have changed much more, and it’s always up to us to decide how or whether to adopt new technologies so that we are staying true to ethical archival work. So our questions when approaching OCR technologies are along the lines of: Is it producing accurate results? Does it fit into a sustainable workflow? Does it support meaningful access to archival materials? 

Archivists have a few different values statements and codes of ethics to look to, but the one that we considered in our paper was the Society of American Archivists’ Core Values Statement and Code of Ethics. The Society of American Archivists is the largest and oldest professional association for archivists in the US, and it does a lot to guide the work of archivists, including developing and maintaining this values statement and code of ethics, which is based on the collective wisdom and experience of generations of archivists, but is also meant to reflect our current values, priorities, pressures, and so on. So if we’re thinking about how different OCR technologies fit with archival values and ethics, then the SAA Core Values Statement and Code of Ethics can serve as sort of evaluation criteria for different kinds of OCR tools.

There are four core sets of values from the SAA framework that we focused on for this assessment of OCR tools: authenticity and integrity, transparency and accountability, access and equity, and responsible stewardship and sustainability.

Authenticity and integrity in archival work refer to the archivist’s responsibility to maintain the trustworthiness of the historical record. When possible, materials that we hold in our collections have to retain their original content, context, and value as evidence of past activities. If we’re using OCR to recognize text in our archival materials, then the OCR’s output should reflect the true content of the documents, including any errors or imperfections that the original documents might have. If the OCR reads something erroneously, then it should be clear that that is an error. The OCR tool should not be trying to make corrections to original materials, making up words when it’s not sure, covering up errors, or otherwise fabricating text that isn’t there. 

Transparency and accountability means that archivists should clearly document their processes, make decisions visible, promote professional accountability, and maintain trust and understanding with non-archivists. There are some historical materials that we are legally obligated to keep away from the public, but otherwise, everything we hold and do should be clear and understandable. When it comes to using OCR, we want to be able to explain and document exactly how OCR was performed: the tool we used, how we decided to use that tool, what the tool does and does not do, where errors might arise, what errors look like, and so on.

Access and equity refer to our work making archival materials discoverable and accessible, trying to overcome technical and economic barriers when possible. We want the public to find the documents they’re interested in and then have access to that content, even if they can’t travel to us, even if they can’t directly view or visually interpret the item for one reason or another - that’s the ideal, anyway. OCR can allow for full-text keyword searching of archival documents, and that full text can also be used with assistive technologies, such as screen readers. Accurate OCR, without fabrications or other unrecognized errors, can support equitable access to the historical record.

Finally, archivists have to make decisions about technology options with sustainability and stewardship in mind. Sustainability in this context can mean choosing options that are less environmentally harmful than others, but it also means using financial, technical, and human resources in a way that will be sustainable for the institution over the long term. And this relates to archivists’ ability to properly steward the materials in our collections, because unsustainable practices threaten long-term stewardship. When it comes to OCR tools, archivists need to consider whether the tool and its outputs are supportable with resources available both now and other the archival timescale, which is generally at least decades, if not centuries. Digital tools are frequently modified, and archivists should try to have enough control over their tools that they can maintain and migrate them.

The sustainability question gets at a bigger issue in the archival world, which is that archivists are usually dependent on some kind of service from vendors or proprietary technologies - we usually don’t have the resources to do or create absolutely everything in-house - and sometimes those services prove to be unsustainable, which puts archival materials or projects at risk. One example is cloud storage - many archival institutions choose to keep their digital records in the cloud, using services such as Amazon Web Services. Once you become reliant on a service, you have given over some degree of power and control. AWS seems pretty reliable now, but in the past it has had issues with unpredictable pricing and confusing mechanisms to actually retrieve your digital records. Google Workspace for Education used to offer free cloud storage to higher ed institutions, but then in 2022 they capped free storage at 100 TB. In another kind of case, an institution might become completely reliant on a proprietary digital collections platform and have a lot of trouble migrating out their content when they don’t want to work with that platform anymore. All of this is to say that becoming dependent on proprietary technology can lead to “rug pulls” that affect archivists and archival collections negatively. This is a sustainability issue, and it’s relevant to OCR because there are a lot of proprietary OCR tools out there that archivists could become dependent on. When choosing which OCR tools we want to use as archivists, we want them to be accurate, transparent, and documentable, but we also want to try to invest our resources in tools that will be helpful and available in a sustainable workflow.

As Andrew has talked about and will go into greater detail about shortly, archivists and librarians have choices about which OCR tools to use, including choices between LLM-based tools and non-LLM-based tools. Each kind of tool has its own advantages and drawbacks and quirks, and each one will fit differently against archival ethics as expressed in the SAA’s values statement and code of ethics. Our case study gets into the construction of an OCR tool that is an alternative to commercial and proprietary LLM OCR tools, and why we chose this direction.
